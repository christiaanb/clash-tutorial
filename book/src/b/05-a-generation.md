# A generation

Chapter 2 wrote the rule for one cell: given whether that cell is alive and how many of its neighbours are, it says whether the cell is alive next.
Chapter 4 computed that count for all sixty-four cells at once.
A generation of Life is the one applied to the other, and it is a single line.

## One line

Add this at the bottom of the file:

```haskell
{{#include ../../../code/src/Chapters/Ch05.hs:step}}
```

`zipWith` combines two vectors element by element, and chapter 4 used it at two levels in `addCounts` to add two `Counts` together.
This is the same two levels over two different things.
The outer `zipWith` pairs each of the board's eight rows with the row of counts that belongs to it.
The inner one pairs each of a row's eight cells with that cell's count and hands the two of them to `nextCell`, which chapter 2 wrote and nothing has used since.

`b` appears twice to the right of the `=`: once as the board whose cells are being paired up, and once inside `neighbourCounts b`.
That is one board read in two places, not two boards.
Why reading `b` twice is safe, here and everywhere, is explained in [One value, read in two places](../explanation/purity.md).

## Notice that

**There is no clock in this design, and there has not been one for five chapters.**
`:i step` answers `Board -> Board`, and nothing in that type mentions time.
A whole generation of a sixty-four cell automaton is combinational logic: sixty-four instances of `nextCell` behind the four hundred and forty-eight additions chapter 4 counted, settling between one board and the next the way any block of gates does.
Writing this in VHDL you would have a process by now, with a clock in its sensitivity list and an edge to wait for, because a process is where that language puts anything that happens to a whole array at once.
Here, being an array and being clocked are separate things, and only the first of them has appeared.

**`step (step glider)` is two blocks in series, not two cycles.**
Applying `step` four times describes four copies of the whole generation, each one's output driving the next, which as hardware is four times the logic and four times the delay through it.
At the prompt it is four times almost nothing, because the prompt is evaluating a function rather than building anything.
Chapter 6 spends one register to close that gap and get one copy of `step`, used again every cycle.

**A `Board` and a `Counts` are the same shape, and `zipWith` is what insists on it.**
Chapter 4 ended by saying this chapter would lay one on top of the other, and the line that does it mentions no sizes at all.
Each `zipWith` takes two vectors of the same length and will take nothing else, so eight rows meet eight rows and eight cells meet eight cells, checked before anything runs.
The order is checked too: `nextCell` wants the `Bool` first and the `Unsigned 4` second, so handing `zipWith` the counts before the board does not compile.

## Where this goes

There is a board, there is a rule for advancing it, and every generation so far has been asked for by hand at the prompt.
Chapter 6 puts a register in the way: `step`'s output becomes the board it is given next, one generation per clock cycle, and the design runs by itself.
Leave `clashi` running.
